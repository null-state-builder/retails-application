The headline

A busy store that sells a lot — but keeps almost no profit

In 10 months the store sold ₹2.98 crore. After the cost of goods and running costs, it kept only about ₹13 lakh — roughly 4%. The reason is not selling; it is discounting and buying. One month (January) lost ₹9 lakh by selling below cost in a deep clearance, and the store's biggest-selling brands make almost no money after their discounts. Fix the discounting and the buying, and the same sales can earn many times more.

2 · The money — profit, discount & margin (#4, #15, #16)

Discount is eating the profit — and past 36% off, the store sells at a loss

Every item is bought at about 64% of its tag price — so at full price the margin is ~36%. The moment a discount crosses 36%, the item is sold below cost. In January the average discount was 40% — so the more it sold, the more it lost.

The 36% rule. Because goods cost ~64% of the tag, any discount above 36% sells below cost. Set 36% as a hard ceiling for normal selling — go beyond it only for genuine dead stock you have decided to dump, and know you are paying to remove it.
What discount cost the store: over 10 months, ₹61 lakh was given away in discount — almost the same as the entire gross profit of ₹70 lakh. Pulling the average discount down by even 5 points is worth roughly ₹15 lakh a year, straight to profit.
3 · Brands (#12, #16)

The biggest-selling brands are the worst earners

This is the most surprising finding. The famous men's brands bring the most sales — but after their heavy discounts they make almost no profit, and some sell at a loss. The quiet Indian-wear and value brands make 35–41% margin at near-full price. The store's money is tied up in the wrong brands.

The real problem: wrong mix (#6, #9)

The store is overstocked overall yet out of stock on 1,184 fast items — mostly t-shirts, shirts, jeans, kurti sets and tops that sold in May–June and are now at zero. Money is locked in slow styles while the fast ones run dry. This is a buying-and-replenishment problem, not a quantity problem.

7 · What we could not measure — and why

Being straight about the limits

  • True conversion rate (part of #3) — not possible. "Conversion" means sales ÷ people who walked in. There is no footfall/door-counter data in any file, so we can only show repeat vs new customers, not a conversion rate. A simple entry counter would unlock this.
  • Profit figures are close estimates, not audited. The sales files have no cost column, so cost of goods is taken from the stock file (every sold item was matched to its cost — a 100% match, so it is reliable, but still an estimate). Running cost uses the formula you gave (7% of sales + rent ₹2.2 L + power ₹1.0 L + misc ₹0.4 L). If the real 7% / fixed costs differ, the profit will shift — but the pattern (good months vs the January loss) holds.
  • Only 10 months, starting September 2025. The data begins when billing started, so there is no Apr–Aug 2025 to compare, and "dead stock" is "not sold in 10 months", not a full year.
  • No colour analysis. The "Colour" column in the sales file is actually a price band (Premium/Medium/Economy), and the stock file has no colour at all — so we cannot analyse colours, only sizes.
  • Stock comes by transfer from head office (the store made no direct purchases), so buying decisions are partly head-office's — treat the buying advice as the store's indent to HO.
8 · What to do — biggest profit first

Six moves

1 Cap the discount — never sell below cost

Set a firm ceiling of 36% off for normal selling (that is the break-even). Plan the Dec–Feb clearance so it does not repeat January's 40%-off, ₹9 lakh loss. Discount deeper only on stock you have decided to dump, and count it as a clearance cost.

↳ Biggest single lever — a 5-point cut in average discount ≈ ₹15 lakh a year.

2 Fix the brand mix

Buy the big western brands (Allen Solly, Van Heusen, Louis Philippe, Arrow, Killer, Levis, Mufti) more tightly and discount them less. Give more space and stock to the high-margin stars — FW, Jay Ambey, Plazer, Yes Mam, Citrus, Jockey.

↳ Same sales, far more profit; less cash tied in slow brands.

3 Clear the aging stock

Run a planned exit for the ₹1.28 crore that hasn't sold in 6+ months — led by old shirts, jeans, t-shirts, trousers, suits and blazers. Bundle, move to other KDPS stores, or clear within the 36% ceiling.

↳ Frees cash and shelf space; lifts stock-turn from 0.7× upward.

4 Buy the right mix, not more stock

Reorder the 1,184 fast items now out of stock (t-shirts, shirts, jeans, kurti sets, tops). Shorten size buys — less men's L/XL depth, less women's XXL, more women's L and Free Size.

↳ Recovers lost sales without adding to the overstock.

5 Capture every customer, then use the list

Get the phone number on every bill (now only 67%). Send WhatsApp reminders to repeat and lapsed customers, and a small perk to the top 5% who bring a quarter of sales.

↳ Near-zero cost; repeat customers already drive 63% of sales.

6 Manage by a monthly dashboard

Track these every month: profit/loss, average discount, margin, stock-turn, dead-stock value, out-of-stock fast items, repeat-customer rate, and price-held by salesperson. These are exactly the numbers in this report.
